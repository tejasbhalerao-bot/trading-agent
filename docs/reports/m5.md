# M5 Report: Backtest and go/no-go

**Finished:** 2026-10-11. **Verdict: no-go.** None of the three checklists makes money once costs are counted. **Awaiting your decision** on what to do next (below).

## The project, in two lines

A system that studies the Indian share market each evening and suggests trades for the next one to three days. It never places an order, it runs on fake money first, and it keeps a permanent record of every decision so it can learn from its own results. Full description: [spec.md](../spec.md).

## What M5 was for

Before spending three to four months paper trading, to test the system on the past: run it over every trading day from 2015 to 2026, exactly as it would have run, and see whether its checklists make money. The plan made this a go/no-go. If no checklist makes money after costs, stop and bring options rather than start paper trading.

## Words used here

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Backtest | Running the system over past years, one evening at a time, using only what was known on each evening. |
| Candidate | A share that met every condition of a checklist on some evening. The backtest judges **every** candidate, not just the few the ₹10,000 account had room for: 63,699 in all. |
| Costs | What a real trade costs on top of the price. **Taxes and fees:** about 0.25% of the trade value for each buy-and-sell (securities transaction tax, stamp duty, exchange fees), at a broker that charges no brokerage. **Flat charges:** about ₹20 per buy-and-sell (depository charge and similar), which is about 1% on a ₹2,000 trade. |
| Per trade | The average gain or loss, as a share of the money put into one trade. |

## The result

### Each checklist, every candidate, April 2015 to October 2026

| Checklist | Candidates | Made money | Per trade, before costs | After taxes and fees | Also after flat charges |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pullback in an uptrend | 52,525 | 48% | **+0.13%** | **−0.12%** | −1.12% |
| Quiet breakout | 10,110 | 44% | −0.23% | −0.48% | −1.48% |
| Gap up that holds | 1,064 | 47% | −0.21% | −0.46% | −1.46% |
| **All together** | 63,699 | 48% | +0.07% | −0.19% | −1.19% |

(Figures count the 1% of trades whose result daily prices can't settle as losses; counting them as wins changes no conclusion.)

- **Only one checklist has an edge before costs**, Pullback in an uptrend. It is real, but tiny: about 13 paise on every ₹100 traded. It made money in 7 of the 12 years.
- **Even the minimum costs are twice that edge.** Taxes and fees alone take 25 paise per ₹100.
- **The flat charges are what really sink a ₹10,000 account.** On a ₹2,000 trade, ₹20 is 1%, about eight times the edge.

### By market mood
The best pairing found was **Pullback in an uptrend when the market is mixed**: +0.26% per trade before costs, and +0.005% after taxes and fees, which is break-even. Nothing is reliably positive after costs in any mood.

(The market mood was missing for April 2015 to February 2016, because NSE renamed its indices that November; it is now fixed. That affects only the split by mood for those ten months. The per-checklist table above includes every candidate whatever the mood, so it is unaffected.)

### What the practice account would have done

Following the system with your limits (₹10,000, 1% risk, 5 positions):

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Start, April 2015 | ₹10,000 |
| End, October 2026, **before any costs** | **₹15,115** (+51% over 11½ years, about 3.7% a year) |
| Taxes and fees on its 4,146 trades | about −₹17,300: more than the whole gain |
| Flat charges on those trades | about −₹82,900 |
| Worst fall along the way | 25% below its highest |
| **For comparison: simply holding the whole market** (Nifty 500) | **₹10,000 → about ₹31,000** |

After taxes and fees alone, the account would have lost money. With flat charges as well, it would have been wiped out.

## Why it fails, in one paragraph

The system makes many small, short trades: about one and a half a day, each about ₹1,700, each aiming for a move of 2–5% within three days. Every trade pays a fixed toll. The patterns win only slightly more than they lose, and the toll is bigger than that small advantage. No amount of learning which checklist to trust can fix this, because the problem is not which trades are chosen but how many and how small they are.

## What the backtest did right

So this "no" can be trusted:
- **Nothing was tuned on profit.** The checklists were chosen in M2 without looking at profits, so this was a genuine first test.
- **No future knowledge:** it ran the real system day by day, and a test proves no decision could see a later price.
- **No hindsight bias in the shares:** the 500 shares were picked month by month from what was trading then, including companies that later collapsed (Jet Airways, DHFL, Yes Bank).
- **Gaps were booked honestly,** at the worse opening price, and unverifiable price breaks were never turned into fake results.
- **Every candidate counted,** not just the lucky few that fitted the account.

## Problems found and fixed during M5

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Some 2020 price files write dates as "13-Jul-20"; older index files as "24/03/2015" | Both formats read; tested |
| An unreadable NSE file would have stopped every evening after it | It now fails only its own day, with the reason recorded |
| NSE's corporate-action list misses some splits and demergers (JSW Steel −90% in 2017, Tata Motors −40% in 2025) | An unexplained move beyond 35% restarts the share's history; trades open across it are "unknown" (decision 0014) |
| NSE renamed its indices in November 2015 ("CNX 500" became "Nifty 500") | Old names recognised; the missing 2015 index days filled in |

**Stored:** market history from 2015 (2,908 trading days, 385 MB), and the backtest's results and all 63,699 judged trades in `data/backtest/` (16 MB). 97 automatic tests pass.

## Decision for you: what next

| Option | What it means | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Stop here** | Close the project with this finding. | Costs nothing more. The finding is solid: short-term trading of this kind doesn't beat costs at this size. | Ends the project. |
| **B. One redesign aimed at beating costs** | A new design pass that tackles costs head-on: **far fewer trades, each aiming for a much bigger move**, held for weeks rather than days, so the toll is small next to the move. It would be paired with a **larger account** (₹1 lakh or more) so flat charges fall to about 0.1–0.2%. To avoid fooling ourselves, it is designed using 2015–2020 only and judged **once** on 2021–2026, data it never saw. If it fails that test, stop. | The only levers that can work are exactly these two: bigger moves per trade, and bigger trades. Most of what was built (data, record, learning, report, evening run) carries over. | Changes the spec (it says trades last 1–3 days, on ₹10,000). Success is genuinely uncertain: a fair chance of another "no". About two milestones of work before knowing. |
| C. Paper trade anyway | Go live on paper as designed, with costs ignored. | Tests the machinery and the learning loop on live data. | Eleven years already show it loses after costs; four months of paper trading cannot overturn that. Not recommended. |

**My recommendation: B, with its strict stopping rule. Or A, if you'd rather not spend more on an uncertain outcome.**

I'd only do B with the held-out test as written. Without it, enough tweaking of past data can make almost anything look profitable, and the spec's whole point is a system you can trust with real money. If B's one test on unseen data fails, that is the end: A.

Whatever you choose, the ₹31,000 comparison is worth keeping in mind. Over these 11 years, simply holding an index fund beat every version of this system by a wide margin.
